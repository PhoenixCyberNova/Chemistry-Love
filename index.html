Chemistry Rocks

Class 10 NCERT • Complete Reactions & Colours

Chemistry is the branch of science that deals with the study of matter, its composition, structure, properties, and the changes it undergoes during chemical reactions. It explores how atoms and molecules interact, rearrange, and form new substances.

Chemistry is extremely important because it explains the world around us — from the food we eat and the air we breathe to medicines, fuels, plastics, and metals. Every material and process we use in daily life has a chemical basis. Understanding chemistry helps us invent new materials, solve environmental problems, develop medicines, and improve technology. In short, chemistry is the central science that connects physics, biology, and the real world.

Chapter 1

Chemical Reactions and Equations
Burning of Magnesium Ribbon Combination
2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s)
Magnesium ribbon burns with a dazzling white flame in air (or oxygen) to form magnesium oxide, a white powder. The ribbon is cleaned with sandpaper before burning to remove the oxide layer. This is a classic example of a combination reaction and also an oxidation reaction.
Formation of Slaked Lime Combination + Exothermic
CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + Heat
Quick lime (calcium oxide) reacts vigorously with water to form slaked lime (calcium hydroxide) and a large amount of heat is released. This is an exothermic combination reaction. Slaked lime is used for whitewashing walls.
Whitewashing Reaction Combination
Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l)
When carbon dioxide from air reacts with the thin layer of slaked lime on walls, a shiny white layer of calcium carbonate is formed. This gives the walls a bright finish after 2–3 days. Marble also has the formula CaCO₃.
Combustion of Methane (Natural Gas) Combination + Exothermic
CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(g) + Heat
Methane burns in excess of oxygen to form carbon dioxide and water vapour, releasing a large amount of heat. This is the reaction that occurs when natural gas is used as fuel.
Respiration (Biological Oxidation) Exothermic
C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(aq) → 6CO₂(aq) + 6H₂O(l) + Energy
Glucose present in our body is oxidised by oxygen to produce carbon dioxide, water and energy. Although this is a biological process, chemically it is similar to combustion and is highly exothermic. The energy is used by living cells.
Formation of Water Combination
2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)
Hydrogen gas burns in oxygen (or air) with a blue flame to form water. This is a classic combination reaction and is highly exothermic.
Thermal Decomposition of Calcium Carbonate Decomposition
CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g)   (on heating)
When limestone (calcium carbonate) is heated strongly, it decomposes to form quick lime (calcium oxide) and carbon dioxide gas. This is an important industrial process for manufacturing cement and quick lime.
Decomposition of Ferrous Sulphate Decomposition
2FeSO₄(s) → Fe₂O₃(s) + SO₂(g) + SO₃(g)   (on heating)
Green crystals of ferrous sulphate (FeSO₄·7H₂O) lose water on heating and then the anhydrous salt decomposes to form reddish-brown ferric oxide, sulphur dioxide and sulphur trioxide. A characteristic smell of burning sulphur is observed.
Thermal Decomposition of Lead Nitrate Decomposition
2Pb(NO₃)₂(s) → 2PbO(s) + 4NO₂(g) + O₂(g)   (on heating)
White lead nitrate crystals on heating decompose to form yellow lead oxide, reddish-brown nitrogen dioxide gas and oxygen. The emission of brown fumes of NO₂ is a clear indication of this reaction.
Electrolysis of Water Electrolytic Decomposition
2H₂O(l) → 2H₂(g) + O₂(g)   (electric current)
When electric current is passed through acidified water, it decomposes into hydrogen and oxygen gases. Hydrogen is collected at the cathode (twice the volume) and oxygen at the anode. This is a classic example of electrolytic decomposition.
Photolytic Decomposition of Silver Chloride Photolytic
2AgCl(s) → 2Ag(s) + Cl₂(g)   (in sunlight)
White silver chloride turns grey when exposed to sunlight because it decomposes into silver and chlorine. This reaction is used in black-and-white photography. Silver bromide also shows similar behaviour.
Photolytic Decomposition of Silver Bromide Photolytic
2AgBr(s) → 2Ag(s) + Br₂(g)   (in sunlight)
Pale yellow silver bromide also decomposes in sunlight to form silver and bromine. This is the basis of photographic films.
Iron + Copper Sulphate Displacement
Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)
When an iron nail is dipped in blue copper sulphate solution, iron being more reactive displaces copper. The blue colour of the solution fades and turns pale green (due to FeSO₄) and a reddish-brown coating of copper is deposited on the iron nail.
Zinc + Copper Sulphate Displacement
Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)
Zinc is more reactive than copper, so it displaces copper from copper sulphate solution. The blue colour disappears and reddish-brown copper is deposited.
Zinc + Silver Nitrate Displacement
Zn(s) + 2AgNO₃(aq) → Zn(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2Ag(s)
Zinc displaces silver from silver nitrate solution because zinc is higher in the reactivity series than silver. Greyish-white silver is deposited.
Aluminium + Copper Chloride Displacement
2Al(s) + 3CuCl₂(aq) → 2AlCl₃(aq) + 3Cu(s)
Aluminium being more reactive displaces copper from copper chloride solution. The blue-green colour of CuCl₂ fades and copper metal is deposited.
Lead + Copper Chloride Displacement
Pb(s) + CuCl₂(aq) → PbCl₂(aq) + Cu(s)
Lead displaces copper from copper chloride solution as it is more reactive than copper.
Barium Chloride + Sodium Sulphate Double Displacement (Precipitation)
BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)
When solutions of barium chloride and sodium sulphate are mixed, a white precipitate of barium sulphate is formed immediately. This is a classic precipitation reaction (double displacement).
Sodium Sulphate + Barium Chloride Double Displacement
Na₂SO₄(aq) + BaCl₂(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)
Same reaction as above. White insoluble barium sulphate precipitates out while sodium chloride remains in solution.
Lead Nitrate + Potassium Iodide Double Displacement
Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2KNO₃(aq)
Mixing lead nitrate and potassium iodide solutions produces a bright yellow precipitate of lead iodide. This is one of the most visually striking precipitation reactions in Class 10.
Sodium Hydroxide + Hydrochloric Acid Neutralisation
NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)
This is a neutralisation reaction (a special case of double displacement). Acid and base react to form salt and water. Heat is also liberated.
Oxidation of Copper Oxidation
2Cu(s) + O₂(g) → 2CuO(s)
When copper is heated in air, it reacts with oxygen to form black copper(II) oxide. Copper is oxidised and oxygen is reduced.
Reduction of Copper Oxide Reduction
CuO(s) + H₂(g) → Cu(s) + H₂O(l)
Black copper oxide is reduced to reddish-brown copper metal when hydrogen gas is passed over it. Hydrogen acts as a reducing agent and itself gets oxidised to water.
Zinc + Dilute Sulphuric Acid Displacement + Redox
Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)
Zinc reacts with dilute sulphuric acid to liberate hydrogen gas (which burns with a pop sound) and forms zinc sulphate. Zinc is oxidised and H⁺ ions are reduced.
Iron + Steam Redox
3Fe(s) + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄(s) + 4H₂(g)
Red hot iron reacts with steam to form iron(II,III) oxide (Fe₃O₄) and hydrogen gas. This is an important redox reaction.
Corrosion of Iron (Rusting) Oxidation
4Fe(s) + 3O₂(g) + xH₂O(l) → 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂O(s)
Iron reacts with oxygen and moisture present in air to form hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust), which is reddish-brown. Rusting is a slow oxidation process that damages iron structures.

Chapter 2

Acids, Bases and Salts
Zinc + Dilute Hydrochloric Acid Acid + Metal
Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)
Zinc reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to form zinc chloride and hydrogen gas. The hydrogen gas burns with a characteristic ‘pop’ sound when a burning matchstick is brought near it. This confirms the presence of hydrogen.
Zinc + Dilute Sulphuric Acid Acid + Metal
Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)
Zinc granules react with dilute sulphuric acid to liberate hydrogen gas and form zinc sulphate. This is one of the most common laboratory methods to prepare hydrogen gas.
Magnesium + Dilute Hydrochloric Acid Acid + Metal
Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)
Magnesium reacts vigorously with dilute hydrochloric acid to produce magnesium chloride and hydrogen gas. The reaction is faster than that of zinc.
Iron + Dilute Hydrochloric Acid Acid + Metal
Fe(s) + 2HCl(aq) → FeCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)
Iron reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to form ferrous chloride and hydrogen gas. The reaction is slower compared to magnesium and zinc.
Sodium Carbonate + Hydrochloric Acid Acid + Carbonate
Na₂CO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
Sodium carbonate (washing soda) reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to form sodium chloride, water and carbon dioxide gas. The CO₂ gas turns lime water milky.
Sodium Hydrogen Carbonate + Hydrochloric Acid Acid + Hydrogen Carbonate
NaHCO₃(s) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
Baking soda reacts with hydrochloric acid to liberate carbon dioxide. This reaction is used in soda-acid fire extinguishers.
Calcium Carbonate + Hydrochloric Acid Acid + Carbonate
CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
Marble chips or limestone react with dilute hydrochloric acid to produce carbon dioxide gas, calcium chloride and water. This is a common laboratory test for carbonates.
Lime Water Test for CO₂ Test Reaction
Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l)
Carbon dioxide turns lime water (calcium hydroxide solution) milky due to the formation of insoluble calcium carbonate. This is the standard confirmatory test for CO₂.
Excess CO₂ with Lime Water Further Reaction
CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g) → Ca(HCO₃)₂(aq)
When excess carbon dioxide is passed through the milky lime water, the milkiness disappears because insoluble calcium carbonate is converted into soluble calcium hydrogen carbonate.
Sodium Hydroxide + Hydrochloric Acid Neutralisation
NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)
Strong base reacts with strong acid to form salt (sodium chloride) and water. Heat is evolved. This is a classic neutralisation reaction.
Potassium Hydroxide + Nitric Acid Neutralisation
KOH(aq) + HNO₃(aq) → KNO₃(aq) + H₂O(l)
Potassium hydroxide neutralises nitric acid to form potassium nitrate and water.
Sodium Hydroxide + Sulphuric Acid Neutralisation
2NaOH(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → Na₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l)
Two molecules of sodium hydroxide are required to completely neutralise one molecule of sulphuric acid, forming sodium sulphate and water.
Copper Oxide + Hydrochloric Acid Acid + Metal Oxide
CuO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CuCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l)
Black copper oxide reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to form bluish-green copper chloride solution and water. Metal oxides are basic in nature and neutralise acids.
Copper Oxide + Sulphuric Acid Acid + Metal Oxide
CuO(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → CuSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l)
Copper oxide dissolves in dilute sulphuric acid to form blue copper sulphate solution.
Calcium Hydroxide + Carbon Dioxide Base + Non-metal Oxide
Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l)
Non-metal oxides are acidic. Carbon dioxide (acidic oxide) reacts with calcium hydroxide (base) to form salt and water.
Sodium Hydroxide + Carbon Dioxide Base + Non-metal Oxide
2NaOH(aq) + CO₂(g) → Na₂CO₃(aq) + H₂O(l)
Sodium hydroxide absorbs carbon dioxide to form sodium carbonate and water. This reaction is used to remove CO₂ from air in closed systems.
Zinc + Sodium Hydroxide Base + Metal
Zn(s) + 2NaOH(aq) → Na₂ZnO₂(aq) + H₂(g)
Zinc reacts with hot concentrated sodium hydroxide to form sodium zincate and hydrogen gas. Zinc is an amphoteric metal.
Aluminium + Sodium Hydroxide Base + Metal
2Al(s) + 2NaOH(aq) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaAlO₂(aq) + 3H₂(g)
Aluminium reacts with sodium hydroxide to form sodium aluminate and hydrogen gas. This shows the amphoteric nature of aluminium.
Chlor-Alkali Process Electrolysis
2NaCl(aq) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + Cl₂(g) + H₂(g)
When electricity is passed through an aqueous solution of sodium chloride (brine), it decomposes to form sodium hydroxide, chlorine gas (at anode) and hydrogen gas (at cathode). This is called the chlor-alkali process.
Formation of Bleaching Powder Important Salt
Ca(OH)₂(s) + Cl₂(g) → CaOCl₂(s) + H₂O(l)
Bleaching powder (calcium oxychloride) is prepared by the action of chlorine on dry slaked lime. It is used for bleaching cotton and paper and for disinfecting drinking water.
Formation of Baking Soda Important Salt
NaCl + H₂O + CO₂ + NH₃ → NH₄Cl + NaHCO₃
Sodium hydrogen carbonate (baking soda) is produced by the Solvay process. It is used in baking, as an antacid, and in soda-acid fire extinguishers.
Heating of Baking Soda Decomposition
2NaHCO₃(s) → Na₂CO₃(s) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)
On heating, baking soda decomposes to form sodium carbonate, water and carbon dioxide. This reaction helps cakes and bread to rise.
Formation of Washing Soda Important Salt
Na₂CO₃ + 10H₂O → Na₂CO₃·10H₂O
Anhydrous sodium carbonate is recrystallised to form washing soda (sodium carbonate decahydrate). It is used in glass, soap and paper industries and for removing permanent hardness of water.
Plaster of Paris from Gypsum Important Salt
CaSO₄·2H₂O → CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O   (373 K)
Gypsum is heated at 373 K to form Plaster of Paris (calcium sulphate hemihydrate). On mixing with water it again forms gypsum and sets into a hard mass. Used for making casts and for setting fractured bones.
Setting of Plaster of Paris Setting Reaction
CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O
When Plaster of Paris is mixed with water, it rehydrates and forms gypsum, which sets into a hard solid mass.

Chapter 3

Metals and Non-metals
Sodium + Oxygen Metal + Oxygen
4Na(s) + O₂(g) → 2Na₂O(s)
Sodium burns in oxygen with a yellow flame to form sodium oxide. Sodium is highly reactive and is stored under kerosene to prevent reaction with air and moisture.
Magnesium + Oxygen Metal + Oxygen
2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s)
Magnesium burns with a dazzling white flame to form white magnesium oxide. Magnesium oxide is basic in nature and turns red litmus blue.
Aluminium + Oxygen Metal + Oxygen
4Al(s) + 3O₂(g) → 2Al₂O₃(s)
Aluminium forms a thin protective layer of aluminium oxide on its surface which prevents further oxidation. This is why aluminium appears unreactive.
Zinc + Oxygen Metal + Oxygen
2Zn(s) + O₂(g) → 2ZnO(s)
Zinc burns in air to form zinc oxide. Zinc oxide is amphoteric in nature.
Iron + Oxygen Metal + Oxygen
3Fe(s) + 2O₂(g) → Fe₃O₄(s)
Iron filings burn in oxygen to form iron(II,III) oxide (magnetic oxide of iron).
Copper + Oxygen Metal + Oxygen
2Cu(s) + O₂(g) → 2CuO(s)
Copper on heating in air forms black copper(II) oxide. The reddish-brown colour of copper changes to black.
Aluminium Oxide + Hydrochloric Acid Amphoteric Oxide
Al₂O₃(s) + 6HCl(aq) → 2AlCl₃(aq) + 3H₂O(l)
Aluminium oxide reacts with acids to form salt and water, showing basic character.
Aluminium Oxide + Sodium Hydroxide Amphoteric Oxide
Al₂O₃(s) + 2NaOH(aq) → 2NaAlO₂(aq) + H₂O(l)
Aluminium oxide also reacts with bases to form sodium aluminate and water, showing acidic character. Hence it is amphoteric.
Zinc Oxide + Hydrochloric Acid Amphoteric Oxide
ZnO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l)
Zinc oxide reacts with acids to form zinc chloride and water.
Zinc Oxide + Sodium Hydroxide Amphoteric Oxide
ZnO(s) + 2NaOH(aq) → Na₂ZnO₂(aq) + H₂O(l)
Zinc oxide reacts with sodium hydroxide to form sodium zincate. This proves the amphoteric nature of ZnO.
Potassium + Water Metal + Water
2K(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2KOH(aq) + H₂(g) + Heat
Potassium reacts violently with cold water. The hydrogen gas produced catches fire. Potassium is stored under kerosene.
Sodium + Water Metal + Water
2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g) + Heat
Sodium reacts vigorously with cold water to form sodium hydroxide and hydrogen gas. The reaction is highly exothermic and hydrogen often catches fire.
Calcium + Water Metal + Water
Ca(s) + 2H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g)
Calcium reacts with water less vigorously than sodium. The hydrogen gas sticks to the surface of calcium and makes it float.
Magnesium + Hot Water Metal + Water
Mg(s) + 2H₂O(l) → Mg(OH)₂(aq) + H₂(g)
Magnesium reacts with hot water to form magnesium hydroxide and hydrogen. It does not react with cold water.
Aluminium + Steam Metal + Steam
2Al(s) + 3H₂O(g) → Al₂O₃(s) + 3H₂(g)
Aluminium reacts with steam to form aluminium oxide and hydrogen gas.
Iron + Steam Metal + Steam
3Fe(s) + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄(s) + 4H₂(g)
Red hot iron reacts with steam to form iron(II,III) oxide and hydrogen gas. This reaction was earlier used for the commercial production of hydrogen.
Magnesium + Dilute HCl Metal + Acid
Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)
Magnesium reacts rapidly with dilute hydrochloric acid to liberate hydrogen gas.
Aluminium + Dilute HCl Metal + Acid
2Al(s) + 6HCl(aq) → 2AlCl₃(aq) + 3H₂(g)
Aluminium reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to form aluminium chloride and hydrogen.
Zinc + Dilute HCl Metal + Acid
Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)
Zinc reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to produce hydrogen gas which burns with a pop sound.
Iron + Dilute HCl Metal + Acid
Fe(s) + 2HCl(aq) → FeCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)
Iron reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to form ferrous chloride and hydrogen gas. The reaction is slower than zinc.
Zinc + Dilute H₂SO₄ Metal + Acid
Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)
Zinc reacts with dilute sulphuric acid to form zinc sulphate and hydrogen. Copper, silver and gold do not react with dilute acids.
Iron + Copper Sulphate Displacement
Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)
Iron being more reactive than copper displaces copper from copper sulphate solution. Blue colour of the solution changes to pale green and reddish-brown copper is deposited.
Zinc + Copper Sulphate Displacement
Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)
Zinc displaces copper from copper sulphate solution. The blue colour disappears.
Zinc + Iron Sulphate Displacement
Zn(s) + FeSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Fe(s)
Zinc is more reactive than iron and displaces it from iron sulphate solution.
Copper + Silver Nitrate Displacement
Cu(s) + 2AgNO₃(aq) → Cu(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2Ag(s)
Copper displaces silver from silver nitrate solution because copper is more reactive than silver. Greyish silver is deposited and the solution turns blue.
Thermit Reaction Displacement (Highly Exothermic)
Fe₂O₃(s) + 2Al(s) → 2Fe(l) + Al₂O₃(s) + Heat
Aluminium reduces iron(III) oxide to molten iron. The reaction is highly exothermic and is used for welding railway tracks (thermit welding).
Roasting of Zinc Sulphide Roasting
2ZnS(s) + 3O₂(g) → 2ZnO(s) + 2SO₂(g)
Zinc sulphide ore is heated in excess air to convert it into zinc oxide. This process is called roasting.
Calcination of Zinc Carbonate Calcination
ZnCO₃(s) → ZnO(s) + CO₂(g)
Zinc carbonate is heated in limited supply of air to obtain zinc oxide. This process is called calcination.
Reduction of Zinc Oxide Reduction
ZnO(s) + C(s) → Zn(s) + CO(g)
Zinc oxide is reduced to zinc metal by heating with carbon (coke). Carbon acts as a reducing agent.
Formation of Ionic Compound (Sodium Chloride) Ionic Bond
2Na(s) + Cl₂(g) → 2NaCl(s)
Sodium (metal) loses one electron to form Na⁺ and chlorine (non-metal) gains one electron to form Cl⁻. The electrostatic force of attraction between them forms the ionic compound sodium chloride.

Chapter 4

Carbon and its Compounds
Combustion of Methane Combustion
CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(g) + Heat + Light
Methane burns in sufficient oxygen with a clean blue flame to form carbon dioxide and water, releasing a large amount of heat and light. This is the reaction that occurs when natural gas is used as fuel.
Combustion of Ethanol Combustion
C₂H₅OH(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(g) + Heat + Light
Ethanol burns completely in air to form carbon dioxide and water. Saturated hydrocarbons and alcohols generally burn with a clean blue flame when oxygen supply is sufficient.
Combustion of Carbon Combustion
C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + Heat + Light
Carbon burns in oxygen (or air) to form carbon dioxide, releasing heat and light.
Oxidation of Ethanol Oxidation
CH₃CH₂OH → CH₃COOH   (Alkaline KMnO₄ / Acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ + Heat)
Ethanol is oxidised to ethanoic acid when heated with alkaline potassium permanganate or acidified potassium dichromate. The purple colour of KMnO₄ disappears. This is an important oxidation reaction.
Hydrogenation of Ethene Addition
CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ → CH₃–CH₃   (Ni or Pd catalyst)
Unsaturated hydrocarbons add hydrogen in the presence of a catalyst (nickel or palladium) to form saturated hydrocarbons. This reaction is called hydrogenation and is used to convert vegetable oils into vanaspati ghee.
Addition of Hydrogen to Ethyne Addition
CH≡CH + 2H₂ → CH₃–CH₃   (Ni catalyst)
Ethyne (acetylene) adds two molecules of hydrogen in the presence of nickel catalyst to form ethane.
Chlorination of Methane Substitution
CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl   (in sunlight)
In the presence of sunlight, chlorine replaces one hydrogen atom of methane to form chloromethane and hydrogen chloride. This is a substitution reaction. Further substitution can occur to form CH₂Cl₂, CHCl₃ and CCl₄.
Ethanol + Sodium Reaction with Metal
2C₂H₅OH + 2Na → 2C₂H₅ONa + H₂(g)
Ethanol reacts with sodium to form sodium ethoxide and hydrogen gas. This reaction is used to detect the presence of alcoholic –OH group.
Dehydration of Ethanol Dehydration
CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O   (Conc. H₂SO₄, 443 K)
When ethanol is heated with excess concentrated sulphuric acid at 443 K, it undergoes dehydration to form ethene. Concentrated H₂SO₄ acts as a dehydrating agent.
Ethanoic Acid + Sodium Hydroxide Neutralisation
CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O
Ethanoic acid reacts with sodium hydroxide to form sodium ethanoate (sodium acetate) and water. This is a neutralisation reaction.
Ethanoic Acid + Sodium Carbonate Acid + Carbonate
2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂
Ethanoic acid reacts with sodium carbonate to liberate carbon dioxide gas, which turns lime water milky. This confirms the acidic nature of ethanoic acid.
Ethanoic Acid + Sodium Hydrogen Carbonate Acid + Hydrogen Carbonate
CH₃COOH + NaHCO₃ → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂
Ethanoic acid reacts with baking soda to produce carbon dioxide, sodium ethanoate and water. This reaction is used in baking and in soda-acid fire extinguishers.
Esterification Reaction Esterification
CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O   (Conc. H₂SO₄)
Ethanoic acid reacts with ethanol in the presence of a few drops of concentrated sulphuric acid to form a sweet-smelling ester (ethyl ethanoate) and water. This reaction is reversible and is called esterification.
Saponification Reaction Saponification
CH₃COOC₂H₅ + NaOH → CH₃COONa + C₂H₅OH
When an ester is heated with sodium hydroxide, it gives back the alcohol and the sodium salt of the carboxylic acid. This reaction is called saponification and is used in the preparation of soap.
